## Why sort by claim type

LinkedIn screening questions look endlessly varied — a hospital asks about BLS certification, a logistics firm about CDL endorsements, a startup about years with Postgres. Trying to memorise answers per industry doesn't scale and doesn't survive contact with the next unfamiliar field.

What does scale is noticing that every screening question is one of a small number of *kinds of claim*, and each kind has its own correct handling. A licence question and a "are you willing to commute" question look identical on screen — both are Yes/No radios — but one asserts a verifiable fact about the world and the other states a preference. Answering the second optimistically is fine. Answering the first optimistically is a lie that surfaces at the background check.

Sort first, then answer.

## Type 1: Factual claims

**What it is.** A statement that is objectively true or false about the applicant and is checkable by the employer: licences, registrations, certifications, degrees, clearances, work authorization, employment at a named company, criminal history, age or right-to-work minimums.

**How to answer.** From the profile only. If the profile lists the credential as active and valid in the relevant region, answer yes. If it's absent, expired, or valid in a different jurisdiction, the honest answer is no — and since a no on a gating credential usually ends the application anyway, skipping the listing and noting it is the better outcome than submitting a doomed or false application.

**Never** infer a credential from adjacent facts. Ten years of nursing does not imply a current licence in the state being applied to. A computer science degree does not imply a security clearance. Passing a certification exam does not imply the certification is unexpired.

**The region trap.** Credentials are jurisdictional in a way that's easy to miss when working fast. An RN licensed in Ohio applying to a Texas hospital, an accountant with an Indian CA applying to a US CPA role, a solicitor admitted in England applying in New York, a CDL holder applying across a national border — each is a *no* to "do you hold the required licence," even though the person is unmistakably qualified. Many US states have compact or reciprocity arrangements that change this answer; that's a question for the person, not a call to make mid-run.

**Flag the jurisdiction gap; don't try to resolve it.** Noticing that someone's licence may not travel is genuinely valuable and worth raising prominently. Explaining the fee, the processing time, the specific rule number, or whether their particular licence auto-converted is a different act, and it's where confident-sounding wrong answers do real harm — these details vary by state and year, and someone planning a move around them needs the licensing board's answer, not a recalled one. Name the gap, say what it turns on, and point them at the authority that can settle it.

**When the profile is silent.** Skip the listing, note which credential was missing, and mention it in the report. If the same credential blocks several good listings, that's worth surfacing prominently — it may be a one-line profile addition that unblocks a dozen applications.

## Type 2: Preference and willingness

**What it is.** Anything asking what the applicant is *willing to do*: commuting, relocating, travel percentage, night or weekend shifts, on-call rotations, background checks, drug screening, start dates, comfort with a stated salary range.

**How to answer.** From the profile's willingness section. Where the profile is silent, use their stated default posture — most people prefer a yes here, because these questions are usually filters rather than commitments and a no ends the application before a human sees it. Answering yes to "willing to relocate" doesn't sign a lease; it keeps a conversation open.

**Where the default doesn't apply.** Two cases deserve a real check rather than an automatic yes:

- *Hard nos the person stated.* If someone said no night shifts because of childcare, a yes to "available for night shift" wastes everyone's time and lands them in an interview for a job they'll decline. Honour the hard no and skip.
- *Questions that are really factual in disguise.* "Are you able to lift 50 pounds" and "do you have reliable transportation" read as willingness but assert capability. Answer from what the person actually told you.

## Type 3: Fuzzy self-assessment

**What it is.** "How many years of experience do you have with X." Proficiency scales. "Which best describes your experience with Y." Nobody can answer these precisely, employers know it, and the numbers mostly get used as crude filters.

**How to answer.**

- *Core skills* — the things central to their daily work — get their headline experience figure. A nurse with eight years of experience has eight years of patient care.
- *Adjacent skills* — real but peripheral — get an honest lower figure, or the profile's adjacent default. Inflating every skill to the headline number produces a profile that claims eight years of everything ever invented, which reads as noise to a recruiter and collapses in a technical screen.
- *Never-touched skills* — answer zero rather than skipping. A zero is honest and often survives the filter; a blank blocks submission.
- *Tiered scales* — pick the middle honest tier rather than the most expert-sounding one. "I have professional experience with this" is both more truthful and more credible than "this is my core daily expertise" across fifteen consecutive applications.

**Rounding.** Rounding a genuine seven and a half years to eight is normal human behaviour. Rounding one year to five is a different act. The line is whether the person would say the number out loud in an interview without wincing.

## Type 4: Free text

**What it is.** "Why do you want this role." "Tell us about a relevant project." "Anything else we should know."

**How to answer.** Two or three specific sentences that connect something real from the profile to something real in the listing. Read the listing first — a sentence naming the actual team, product, patient population, or region beats any amount of polish.

Avoid the tells of bulk applications: no "I am writing to express my strong interest," no adjective stacks, no claims the resume doesn't support. If the profile genuinely has nothing relevant to say for a listing, that's a signal the listing may be outside the filter.

**Everything written here is the person speaking.** That makes free text the easiest place to fabricate without noticing, because the fabrications are flattering rather than factual. Character claims the person never made ("reliable," "hardworking," "thrives under pressure"), invented scene-setting ("in a high-volume environment"), and quotes they never said all read as harmless polish and all become things they have to defend in an interview. If a sentence adds something the person didn't tell you, cut it or leave a marked blank for them to fill.

Keep length proportional to the box. A three-line textarea wants three lines.

## Type 5: Administrative

**What it is.** Email, phone, address, current employer, notice period, current and expected pay, LinkedIn URL, how they heard about the role.

**How to answer.** Straight from the profile. Two things to watch:

- *Units on pay.* Forms ask for annual, monthly, hourly, or LPA and rarely label it clearly. Read the field label and any placeholder text, convert from the profile figure, and don't paste an annual number into a monthly field — an accidental order-of-magnitude error here reads as either a typo or an absurd expectation.
- *Notice period units.* Days versus months versus weeks. "2" in a months field is very different from "2" in a days field.

## Deciding which type a question is

When a question doesn't obviously sort, ask: *could an employer check this and catch a false answer?*

- Yes, from a registry, transcript, background check, or previous employer → **factual**, answer from the profile only
- No, it's about what the person would agree to → **preference**, use their default
- Sort of, but only loosely, and everyone's answer is approximate → **fuzzy**, use the honest-number rules

That single question resolves nearly everything, including question types not listed here.

## Worked examples by field

**Healthcare.** "Do you hold an active RN license in the state of Georgia?" → factual, profile only, region-specific. "Are you BLS certified?" → factual, check expiry, these lapse every two years. "Are you available for 12-hour night shifts?" → preference, but honour a stated hard no. "How many years of ICU experience?" → fuzzy, honest number; ICU is a specialty, so don't answer it with general nursing years.

**Trades and construction.** "Do you have an active journeyman electrician licence?" → factual and jurisdictional. "Are you OSHA 30 certified?" → factual. "Can you lift 50 lbs repeatedly?" → capability, from what the person said, not an automatic yes. "Do you own your own tools?" → factual, ask if unknown.

**Transport and logistics.** "Class A CDL with hazmat endorsement?" → factual, and endorsements are separate from class; a Class A without hazmat is a no to this question. "Current DOT medical card?" → factual, expires. "Willing to be out 2 weeks at a time?" → preference with real consequences; honour a stated hard no.

**Legal.** "Admitted to practice in New York?" → factual and jurisdictional; admission elsewhere is a no. "Years of experience in commercial litigation?" → fuzzy, but practice area is specific, so don't answer it with total years of legal practice.

**Finance and accounting.** "Are you a licensed CPA?" → factual; a CA, ACCA, or CPA candidacy is not a CPA. "Series 7?" → factual, and it typically requires active sponsorship, which may make an unsponsored holder's answer no. "Years with SAP?" → fuzzy.

**Education.** "Do you hold a valid state teaching licence?" → factual and jurisdictional. "Are you willing to coach an after-school activity?" → preference. "Years teaching high school mathematics?" → fuzzy, subject and level specific.

**Tech.** "Years of experience with Kubernetes?" → fuzzy, adjacent-skill rules apply. "Do you have an active security clearance?" → factual, and level matters; Secret is not TS/SCI. "Will you require visa sponsorship now or in the future?" → factual, and the "in the future" clause catches people on time-limited status, so answer from the visa details in the profile rather than from current authorization alone.

**Warehouse, retail, hospitality.** "Are you 18 or older?" → factual. "Food handler's card?" → factual, jurisdictional, expires. "Available weekends and holidays?" → preference. "Do you have reliable transportation?" → capability, from the profile.

## When to skip the listing entirely

Skipping is a normal, healthy outcome — not a failure. Skip when:

- A gating factual question can't be answered truthfully as yes
- The profile is silent on a factual question and there's no safe answer
- The listing violates a hard no the person stated
- The role falls outside the agreed filter
- Sponsorship is required and the listing says none is offered, or vice versa
- The form demands a document or detail the profile doesn't have

Record every skip with its reason. The pattern across skips is often the most valuable thing in the final report: a lapsed certification blocking twelve listings, or a location filter quietly excluding most of the market, is a finding the person can act on.
